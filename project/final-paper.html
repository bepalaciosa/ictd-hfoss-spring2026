This is your team's comprehensive analysis of and reflection on your HFOSS community engagement. It should tell the full story of your project: who you engaged with, what you contributed, what you found, and what you learned. Figures, diagrams, and appendices are encouraged and do not count toward the page limit.

Option A: Single Community

Will produce a deep analysis of one HFOSS community (see details in Part 4).

Option B: Comparative Analysis

Will analyze two communities connected by a common theme. Throughout this page, Option B boxes indicate where your paper should differ from Option A.

Required Sections

1. Introduction (1-3 pages)

Introduce your project and set up the rest of the paper. I suggest you write this portion last. You must include:

  1. Background: briefly describe your HFOSS community or communities, including its history, mission, users, and impact (1-2 paragraphs)
  2. Structure: provide an overview of the technical architecture and community governance. For example, the primary programming languages, key maintainers, decision-making process, and how contributions are reviewed (2-4 paragraphs)
  3. Goals & Framework: describe your team's goals and the questions that guided your engagement, and identify the theoretical framework you chose for your critical analysis and why it is relevant to your community (1-2 paragraphs)
Option B

Introduce each community briefly and separately, then explain the common theme connecting them and why a comparative approach adds value beyond studying either community alone.

2. Stakeholder Analysis (2-3 pages)

Build a stakeholder analysis of your HFOSS community, expanding on the approach you practiced in Exercise 4. As before, use the three-question filter to decide which groups belong on your map: Does this group's involvement significantly shape the project's direction or impact? Would the project look or work differently if their needs were centered? Would excluding them from your analysis cause you to miss something important?

Part A: Written Analysis

Your written analysis should address the following. Together, these observations will also serve as the foundation for your critical analysis in Section 4.

  1. Who: Identify all key groups involved in or affected by the project, such as developers, maintainers, funders, end users, and beneficiaries. For each group, briefly describe their role and whether they belong to the contributor community, the target/deployment community, or both. Include at least 8 groups.
  2. Power and influence: Who has decision-making power, and whose voices shape the project's direction? Provide at least one specific example from your engagement that illustrates a power dynamic you observed.
  3. Needs and priorities: Whose needs are centered in the project's design and roadmap? Are there groups whose needs appear underserved or invisible?
  4. Benefits and exclusions: Who benefits from this project, and under what conditions? Who might be excluded, and why?

Part B: Diagram

A visual diagram is required and must be included in the Appendix. Your diagram should convey who the stakeholders are, whether they belong to the contributor community, the target/deployment community, or both, and their relative power or influence.

Option B

You may submit a single combined map and analysis covering both communities. If you do, make sure your diagram clearly labels which stakeholders belong to each community individually and which apply to both. Your written analysis should answer the same seven questions above, but address both communities together where they overlap and separately where they diverge. Include at least 12 groups total across both communities.

3. Contribution Documentation (3-4 pages)

Document what your team attempted, built, and learned through the contribution process. This section should be specific and evidence-based. Link to your actual work wherever possible, and be honest about what went as planned and what didn't.

Every team member must document at least one contribution attempt. Structure this section by contributor: label each subsection with the team member's name, then address the four points below. If you made more than three contributions, describe your three most significant ones.

  1. Contribution Artifacts: Describe your contribution(s). What you did, what problem or enhancement you addressed, and why it mattered to the project. Include links to PRs, commits, issues, or documentation wherever possible.
  2. Process: Describe what tools you used and any challenges you encountered along the way, including how you worked through them.
  3. Community Interactions: Did you receive feedback through a code review, issue thread, or any other community channel? If so, how did you respond, and did it change your approach?

    If you did not receive feedback, reflect on aspects of your contribution where you'd welcome community input, and describe one or two concrete steps you could take to invite that conversation.

  4. Impact & Outcomes: Were your contributions merged or accepted? If not, why do you think that is and what did you learn from that? Reflect on the future potential of your work, even if it wasn't merged.

4. Critical Analysis (4-5 pages)

Choose one framework from the course to guide your analysis:

  • Postcolonial Computing
  • Feminist HCI
  • Social Justice-Oriented Design
  • Asset-Based Community Development (ABCD)
  • Participatory Design / Co-Design
  • Action Research
  • Repair & Maintenance
  • Post-Growth Computing
  • Any other framework covered in class

Begin with a short introductory paragraph (~1 paragraph) explaining your chosen framework in your own words: what it argues, what it highlights, and why it is relevant to your community.

Applying a framework means using its core concepts to interpret your observations, not just describing them again. For example, if you chose Postcolonial Computing and observed in 4.1 that the project is primarily deployed in the Global South but developed in the Global North, the framework would push you to ask: what assumptions about users does that reflect, and whose knowledge and context is being centered or excluded as a result?

Your observations and experiences from Sections 1-3 are your starting point. Throughout the analysis, support your claims with specific evidence from your engagement, your progress logs, and course materials. Make sure to cite all references clearly.

Option A — Single Community

4.1: Observe

Before applying your framework, document your observations. Answer each of the following based on what you found during your engagement:

  1. Context: How does this technology work differently across different settings or user populations? Describe at least two examples.
  2. Sustainability: How is this project sustained financially, socially, and technically? What vulnerabilities did you notice?
  3. Impact: What evidence exists of the project's impact on its intended beneficiaries? How confident can you be in that evidence?

4.2: Evaluate

Using your framework, evaluate the project as a whole. Go beyond description: explain what the framework reveals about what you observed.

  1. What are the project's most significant strengths when viewed through your framework? Be specific.
  2. Where do you see the most important tensions or missed opportunities? What would need to change for the project to better align with the values your framework foregrounds?
  3. What did your framework help you see that you might have missed otherwise? Were there moments where you noticed the framework's own blind spots or limitations?
Option B — Comparative Analysis

4.1: Observe

Before applying your framework, document your observations across both communities. Answer each of the following based on what you found during your engagement:

  1. Context: How does each community's technology work differently across settings or user populations? Where do the two communities diverge most?
  2. Sustainability: How do your two communities' approaches to sustainability compare? Which appears more vulnerable, and why?
  3. Impact: How do the two communities define and measure impact? Do they rely on similar or different forms of evidence?

4.2: Evaluate

Using your framework, evaluate both communities together. Go beyond description: explain what the framework reveals about what you observed.

  1. What are the most significant strengths of each community when viewed through your framework? Where do they differ, and what might account for that?
  2. Where do you see the most important tensions or missed opportunities in each community? Are those tensions related to the same underlying causes, or are they distinct?
  3. What did applying your framework to two communities help you see that studying either one alone would not have? Did the comparison surface any limitations or blind spots in the framework itself?

5. Group Reflection (1-2 pages)

This section is written collaboratively by the whole team. It will be shared (with your permission) as part of a class-wide living document to help future students navigate this class and make meaningful HFOSS contributions.

  1. How did you find and choose your HFOSS community? What would you do differently, and what advice would you give future students on picking a good project to work with?
  2. What did your onboarding process look like? What helped you get started, and what slowed you down?
  3. What practical advice would you give future contributors to this specific project? What do you wish you had known before you started?
  4. How did you manage the workload and pacing across the semester? What strategies helped your team stay on track?
  5. What is one thing that surprised you about contributing to an HFOSS community, and what should future students be prepared for?

Note: Individual reflections and peer feedback are submitted separately as your Project Self-Reflection, the second component of your Project Diary.

6. Conclusion (~1 page)

Wrap up your paper by addressing the following:

  1. Key findings: What are the two or three most important things you learned about your community through your engagement and analysis?
  2. Bigger picture: What does your experience suggest about HFOSS communities more broadly? Is there anything your critical analysis revealed that you think others in the ICTD/HFOSS space should pay attention to?
  3. Looking ahead: Based on what you found, what do you think should happen next, whether for your project, your community, or the field?

Use appendices for supporting material that would interrupt the flow of the main paper: code samples, screenshots of contributions, community conversation logs, or additional visualizations.
