Final HFOSS Community Paper

CS491/591 • Spring 2026 • University of New Mexico

< back to syllabus

Due: Finals Week, Wednesday 5/14, 11:59 PM
Format: 15-20 pages, double-spaced, 12pt font, 1" margins
Weight: 45% of group project grade (see syllabus for overall course grade breakdown)

This is your team's comprehensive analysis of and reflection on your HFOSS community engagement. It should tell the full story of your project: who you engaged with, what you contributed, what you found, and what you learned. Figures, diagrams, and appendices are encouraged and do not count toward the page limit.

Option A teams (single community) will produce a deep analysis of one community. Option B teams (comparative analysis) will analyze 2 communities connected by a common theme. Notes throughout this page indicate where the two options differ.

Required Sections

1. Executive Summary (~1 page)

A concise overview of the entire paper. Cover your project's focus, your key contributions, your main findings, and a brief critical takeaway. Write this last.

2. Introduction (2-3 pages)

Introduce your project and set up the rest of the paper. Include:

  • Background on your HFOSS community or communities: history, mission, users, and impact
  • Overview of technical architecture and community governance
  • Your team's goals and the questions that guided your engagement
  • The theoretical frameworks you applied
  • A brief roadmap of the paper's structure

Option B: Introduce each community briefly, then explain the common theme connecting them and why a comparative approach is valuable.

3. Community Overview (3-4 pages)

  1. Community Health: Assess the community's size, diversity, and activity level. Describe its governance model, communication channels, and norms. How does it welcome (or not welcome) newcomers? Support your assessment with evidence: metrics, screenshots, or conversation excerpts.
  2. Stakeholder Mapping: Identify the key stakeholders, e.g., developers, users, funding sources, beneficiaries. Analyze power dynamics: who has voice and influence? Whose needs are prioritized? Who benefits, and who might be excluded? A diagram is strongly recommended here.
  3. Engagement Process: Describe how you introduced yourselves to the community, the relationships you built, and how you navigated community norms. What was the response from maintainers and contributors? What challenges came up, and how did you address them?

Option B: Analyze each community separately across these three areas, then include a brief comparative synthesis at the end of this section highlighting key similarities and differences.

4. Contribution Documentation (3-4 pages)

What You Contributed

Describe each contribution in detail, including your technical approach and the decisions you made along the way. Be specific about division of labor, i.e., who led what. Include links to PRs, commits, issues, and documentation wherever possible.

Development Process

Walk through your workflow: what tools you used, what technical challenges you encountered and how you solved them, what feedback you received through code review or community interaction, and how you iterated based on that input.

Impact & Outcomes

Were your contributions merged or accepted? If not, why do you think that is and what did you learn from that? Describe the community's response and any tangible impact on the project. Reflect on the future potential of your work, even if it wasn't merged.

Option B: Document contributions to each community separately. In your outcomes discussion, reflect on whether contributing across multiple communities shaped your approach differently than a single-community focus would have.

5. Critical Analysis (4-5 pages)

Choose one or two frameworks from the course:

  • Postcolonial Computing
  • Feminist HCI
  • Social Justice-Oriented Design
  • Asset-Based Community Development (ABCD)
  • Participatory Design / Co-Design
  • Action Research
  • Repair & Maintenance
  • Post-Growth Computing
  • Any other framework covered in class

For each framework, briefly explain it (~1 paragraph), then apply it to analyze the project. Your analysis should address:

  • What does this lens reveal about the project's design, governance, and impact?
  • What assumptions does the project make?
  • Whose knowledge and values are centered?
  • How does the project navigate power dynamics?
  • What are the project's strengths through this lens? What are its tensions or contradictions?

Across your critical analysis, also address the following:

  • Technology as amplifier: what human forces is this technology amplifying?
  • Context: how does this technology work differently across different settings? Give two examples
  • Sustainability: how is this project sustained, and what are its vulnerabilities?
  • Participation: who participates in design and development? Who doesn't, and why? You may limit this to 1-2 examples.
  • Impact: what is the actual impact on intended beneficiaries? How do we know?

Support all claims with evidence from your experience and observations.

Option B: Apply your frameworks comparatively where possible. What does the lens reveal when you look across communities rather than just within one?

6. Group Reflection (1-2 pages)

This section is written collaboratively by the whole team. It will be shared (with your permission) as part of a class-wide living document on HFOSS contribution experiences.

  • How did your team work together? What collaboration strategies worked well?
  • What was difficult about team coordination, and how did you navigate it?
  • How did you divide labor? Was it equitable?
  • What would you advise future teams or future contributors to this project?
  • What does your experience reveal about HFOSS communities more broadly?

NOTE: Individual reflections and peer feedback are submitted separately as your Project Self-Reflection, the second component of your Project Diary.

7. Conclusion (~1 page)

  • Summarize your key findings
  • Reflect on broader implications for ICTD and HFOSS
  • What's next for this project or community?
  • Any final thoughts

8. References

Cite all sources: course readings, project documentation, community conversations, and any external resources. Use a consistent citation style throughout (APA or IEEE, consistent with your other written work in this course).

9. Appendices (optional, not counted toward page limit)

Use appendices for supporting material that would interrupt the flow of the main paper: code samples, screenshots of contributions, community conversation logs, stakeholder maps, or additional visualizations.

Grading

The Final HFOSS Community Paper is worth 45% of your Group Project grade. It is evaluated across six components:

Component Points
Community Analysis 15
Contribution Documentation 15
Critical Analysis 25
Group Reflection 10
Writing Quality 10
Individual Accountability 25
Total 100
  • Community Analysis (15): Thorough, evidence-based, and stakeholder-aware. Goes beyond surface-level description to analyze power, inclusion, and governance.
  • Contribution Documentation (15): Clear, detailed, and honest about outcomes — including contributions that weren't merged or didn't go as planned.
  • Critical Analysis (25): Deep, thoughtful application of at least two course frameworks. Moves beyond summary to genuine critique and insight, supported by evidence from your experience.
  • Group Reflection (10): Honest, collaborative, and specific. Demonstrates genuine collective learning rather than generic takeaways.
  • Writing Quality (10): Clear, organized, and professional. Well-cited, consistent in style, and readable.
  • Individual Accountability (25): Division of labor is transparent throughout the paper. Each team member's contributions are traceable and substantial.
